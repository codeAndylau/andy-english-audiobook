# Part 1 — Small Conversations

## Chapter 1 — The Second Question

A2 · 请求重复、确认时间、提出替代安排

My bag is outside the door. My phone is in my hand.

Apartment 4B.

I check the number again. Then I knock.

A woman opens the door. She has a blue cup in one hand.

“Andy?”

“Yes. Hi. I’m Andy.”

“I’m Maya. Come in.”

I pick up my bag and follow her.

The apartment smells of coffee. There are two chairs by the window and a small table between them. One chair has books on it.

“Sorry about the books,” Maya says. “Your room’s this way.”

The room is small, but it has a desk. I put my bag beside the bed.

“Is it okay?” she asks.

“Yes. It’s nice.”

There are other things I want to ask. Where can I put my food? Does the washing machine work? Why is there a bucket in the kitchen?

But Maya’s phone rings.

She looks at the screen and turns off the sound.

“Sorry. Work.”

“No problem.”

We go back to the kitchen.

“Tea?” she asks.

“Yes, please.”

“Milk?”

“No, thanks.”

Good. I can do this.

Maya puts a cup in front of me. Then she points to the bucket.

“The kitchen tap’s broken. Someone’s coming tomorrow, between eight and twelve. Can you be here? I’ve got to go to the office.”

I hear tomorrow. I hear eight.

“Yes,” I say.

Maya picks up her phone.

“Great. Thank you.”

I look at the bucket. A drop of water falls into it.

Then another.

I have a meeting tomorrow at ten. My office is forty minutes away.

Perhaps the man will come at eight. Perhaps the work will take ten minutes.

Perhaps I should ask.

Maya starts to type.

“Sorry,” I say.

She looks up.

“Could you say that again? The time?”

“Between eight and twelve.”

“Eight and twelve,” I repeat. “So, maybe eleven?”

“Yes. We don’t have an exact time.”

“Oh. I’m sorry. I have to leave at nine.”

Maya puts her phone down.

“Right.”

For a moment, neither of us speaks.

“I can stay until nine,” I say. “Is that okay?”

“Not really. He might come later.”

My face feels hot. I look at my tea.

Then I try another question.

“Can he come in the afternoon?”

Maya looks at her phone.

“I don’t know. Let me ask.”

She types a message. I move my cup away from the edge of the table.

A minute later, her phone makes a sound.

“Two to four,” she says. “I can be home then.”

“So I don’t need to stay?”

“That’s right.”

“Okay. Sorry about that.”

“It’s okay.” She picks up her cup. “Just tell me when you’re not sure.”

Her phone rings again.

“I need to take this. Make yourself at home.”

“Thanks.”

She walks towards her room.

I look at the two chairs by the window. Then I look at the books.

“Maya?”

“Yes?”

“Can I move these?”

“Of course. Put them on the table.”

I move the books and sit down.

My tea is still warm.

### 开口练习

#### 实用表达

- **Could you say that again?** — 没听清时请求重复
- **I have to leave at nine.** — 说明自己的时间限制
- **I can stay until nine.** — 说明可以待到什么时候
- **Can he come in the afternoon?** — 提出替代安排
- **Let me ask.** — 表示需要向别人确认

#### 语言与发音

between eight and twelve 是一个时间范围；until nine 表示一直到九点。把八点至十二点、两点至四点各说一次，再用自己的日程造句。

按意群说：I can STAY / until NINE。先清楚表达时间，再把整句连起来，不用追求快。

#### 理解问题

1. What is broken?
2. Why can’t Andy stay all morning?
3. What is the new time?

#### 角色扮演

你刚搬进公寓，对方想请你等维修人员。先听对方，再用自己的时间回答。

> Someone is coming tomorrow morning. Can you be here?

你的任务：询问具体时间，不要立刻答应。

> Between eight and twelve.

你的任务：说明你九点必须离开。

> Then we need a different time.

你的任务：询问能否改到下午。

#### 换个场景

改成预约送货：你下午两点到五点在家。先说明能在家的时间，再请求对方确认。

#### 脱稿复述

用四句说清楚：Andy 到哪里、答应什么、有什么困难、最后怎样解决。

#### 理解题参考答案（先自己回答）

1. The kitchen tap is broken.
2. He has to leave at nine for a meeting at ten.
3. Between two and four in the afternoon.

## Chapter 2 — Two Shelves

A2 · 询问归属、表达需要、商量共用空间

At six, I go to the small shop near our building.

I buy milk, eggs, tomatoes and some bread. Tomorrow is a workday. I need something for breakfast.

When I get back, Maya’s bedroom door is closed. I can hear her talking.

I take my food into the kitchen.

There are two shelves in the fridge. The top shelf is almost full. There is some cheese, a bowl of rice and three small boxes.

The bottom shelf is empty.

That must be mine.

I put my milk, eggs and tomatoes on it. Then I put the bread on the kitchen table.

Maya comes out of her room.

“Something smells good,” she says.

“The bread’s still warm. Would you like some?”

“Yes, please.”

She gets two plates. I open the bag.

Then she sees the fridge.

“Oh. Is that your food on the bottom shelf?”

“Yes. Is that okay?”

“Sorry. I need that shelf tonight.”

My hand stops inside the bread bag.

“All of it?”

“There’s a cake coming. It’s for someone at work. It’s her last day tomorrow.”

“Oh. I didn’t know.”

“I know. I forgot to tell you.”

I look at my shopping.

“Where can I put my food?”

“Let’s see.”

She opens the fridge and moves two boxes.

“You can use the top shelf.”

I pick up the milk, but the bottle is too tall.

“It doesn’t fit.”

“Try the door.”

There is a large bottle of water in the fridge door.

“Is this yours?” I ask.

“Yes. You can take it out. It doesn’t need to be cold.”

I move the water and put my milk in its place.

The eggs and tomatoes fit on the top shelf.

“What about tomorrow?” I ask. “Can I use the bottom shelf then?”

“Yes. The cake’s leaving with me in the morning.”

“And the top shelf?”

“We can share it.”

I look at the cheese.

“So, we share the shelf, but not all the food?”

Maya smiles.

“Right. Please ask first.”

“Okay. You too.”

“Of course.”

We sit by the window with our bread.

A drop of water falls into the bucket in the kitchen.

“Tomorrow afternoon,” Maya says.

“Between two and four,” I reply.

She nods.

I pass her another piece of bread.

“This is for both of us.”

### 开口练习

#### 实用表达

- **Is this yours?** — 询问东西归谁
- **Can I put this here?** — 请求使用一个位置
- **I need some space.** — 表达空间需求
- **It doesn’t fit.** — 说明大小不合适或放不进去
- **We can share it.** — 提出共用

#### 语言与发音

my milk 和 your milk 后面有名词；mine 和 yours 可以独立使用。说两组：This is my bag. This bag is mine. Is this your cup? Is this yours?

把重点放在物主上：Is this YOURS? / No, it’s MINE。每句说完稍停，模拟真的在等回答。

#### 理解问题

1. Why does Maya need the bottom shelf?
2. Where does Andy put the milk?
3. Do they agree to share all their food?

#### 角色扮演

你在整理厨房，需要先问归属，再表达自己的需要。

> There’s a bag on the table.

你的任务：问这是不是对方的包。

> Yes, it’s mine. Do you need the table?

你的任务：说你需要一点空间放食物。

> You can put your milk on the top shelf.

你的任务：说瓶子放不进去，问能否放冰箱门里。

#### 换个场景

换成办公室：桌上有别人的包，你需要用桌子。用三句话问归属、说明需要、请求把包放到椅子上。

#### 脱稿复述

用四到六句讲清 Andy 买了什么、放在哪里、Maya 需要什么、两人怎样解决。

#### 理解题参考答案（先自己回答）

1. A cake for a coworker is coming.
2. In the fridge door, in place of the water.
3. No. They share the shelf and ask before taking food.

## Chapter 3 — Dinner at Seven

A2 · 确认邀请、改约时间、说明位置

The next morning, the cake leaves the apartment in a large white box. Maya holds it with both hands.

“Can you get the door?” she asks.

I open it for her. There is space in the fridge again.

At work, my meeting finishes late. I eat a sandwich at my desk. At three, Maya sends a photo. There is a new tap in our kitchen. The bucket is gone.

Under the photo, she writes, “Dinner at seven? My friend Rui is coming too.”

“Sounds good,” I write. “Where?”

She sends the name of a restaurant and a map. It is near the station, not far from home.

At six forty, I leave work. On the train, I look at the menu on my phone. There are pictures of noodles, rice and fish. I am very hungry.

Then another message arrives.

“Sorry. Still at work. Can we make it half past seven?”

I look at the train map. I am almost there. I start to write, “No problem.” Then I stop.

I am hungry. Waiting is a small problem. I can say that.

“I’m almost there,” I write. “Can I order something first?”

“Yes, please do. Rui is nearby. I’ll ask him to meet you.”

Outside the station, there are two exits. I choose the wrong one. Across the road, I can see the restaurant’s green sign. There is no crossing near me.

My phone rings.

“Hi, is that Andy? It’s Rui. Where are you?”

“Outside the station.”

“Which exit?”

I look behind me.

“Exit two. There’s a flower shop.”

“Okay. Stay there. I’m coming.”

Five minutes later, a man in a grey jacket raises his hand.

“Andy?”

“Yes. Thanks for finding me.”

“No problem. The crossing is this way.”

We walk to the restaurant together. Rui works with software too. He knows Maya from a design course. We start in English because he asks about the messages in our dinner group. I want to keep trying.

Inside, we find a small table.

“Shall we wait?” Rui asks.

“For the main food, yes. But I’d like something small now.”

“Good. Me too.”

We order a plate of dumplings. Rui asks me about my new room. I tell him about the fridge, the cake and the tap. I forget the word for the thing under the old tap.

“It holds water,” I say. “On the floor.”

“A bucket?”

“Yes. A bucket.”

At seven thirty-five, Maya arrives with her laptop bag.

“Sorry I’m late.”

“We started without you,” Rui says.

“I can see that.”

There is one dumpling left. I move the plate towards her.

“This one’s yours.”

She sits down and takes it.

“Thank you. How was your day?”

I could say, “Fine.” It is a good, safe word.

Instead, I tell them about the meeting that took too long, and the sandwich that was too small. When the waiter arrives, I am still talking.

### 开口练习

#### 实用表达

- **Sounds good. Where?** — 接受邀请并问清地点
- **Can we make it half past seven?** — 商量改时间
- **I’m almost there.** — 说明快到目的地
- **Can I order something first?** — 等待时提出自己的需要
- **There’s a flower shop.** — 用地标说明位置

#### 语言与发音

There is / There’s 用于说明某地有什么：There’s a shop near exit two。用你所在位置附近的两个地标造句。

区分 SEVEN 和 HALF past SEVEN。说三组时间并录下来听，重点是对方能否听清，不用模仿固定语速。

#### 理解问题

1. What time do they first plan to have dinner?
2. Where does Rui find Andy?
3. What do Andy and Rui order before Maya arrives?

#### 角色扮演

朋友晚到，你已经到了餐厅。

> Sorry. Can we meet at half past seven?

你的任务：说你快到了，并问能否先点一点吃的。

> Sure. Where are you now?

你的任务：用出口和一个地标说明位置。

> I’m here. How was your day?

你的任务：用两句真实的话说今天的一件事，别只说 fine。

#### 换个场景

改成看电影前见面：朋友会迟到十五分钟。说明你的位置，并商量是在门口等还是先进去。

#### 脱稿复述

用 first、then、finally 三个连接词讲清晚餐安排如何改变，控制在一分钟。

#### 理解题参考答案（先自己回答）

1. At seven.
2. At exit two, near a flower shop.
3. A plate of dumplings.

## Chapter 4 — The Wrong Order

A2 · 礼貌说明问题、请求更换、确认费用

On Saturday, Rui and I meet for lunch. There is a small café near the river. The tables outside are full, so we sit by the kitchen door.

“English menu?” the waiter asks.

“Yes, please,” I say.

Rui looks up from his phone.

“Still practising?”

“A little.”

“Okay. You order.”

The café has sandwiches, soup and coffee. I choose a chicken sandwich and a hot tea. Rui wants soup and a cold coffee.

When the waiter comes back, I tell him our order. He says it again. I hear the food, but a loud machine starts in the kitchen. I miss the drinks.

I nod.

Ten minutes later, two cold coffees arrive.

I look at the glass in front of me. There is ice inside.

“Did you want coffee?” Rui asks.

“No. Tea.”

“Tell him.”

The waiter is carrying plates to another table. He looks busy.

“It’s okay,” I say. “I can drink this.”

“You can. But is it what you want?”

I look at the glass again. It is a small thing. I do not want to make it a big thing. But I also do not want a cold coffee.

When the waiter passes our table, I raise my hand.

“Excuse me.”

“Yes?”

“I ordered a hot tea. This is coffee.”

He looks at the small screen in his hand.

“I have two coffees here.”

For a moment, I think that is the end of the conversation. The screen says coffee. There is coffee on the table.

Rui waits. He does not speak for me.

“Maybe we didn’t hear each other,” I say. “Could I have tea instead?”

“Of course. I’ll change it.”

“Thank you. Do I need to pay for both?”

“No. Just the tea.”

He takes the glass away. My hands are warm, but not from a drink.

Rui opens his soup.

“That was fine,” he says.

“I wasn’t sure what to say.”

“You said it.”

The waiter brings a cup of tea and puts it carefully beside my sandwich.

“Hot tea,” he says.

“Yes. Thank you.”

We eat. Rui tells me about a problem at work. I understand some of it. When he speaks too fast, I ask him to slow down. He does, but only for a minute. Then he gets excited and goes fast again.

“Wait,” I say. “What happened after the test?”

He puts down his spoon and starts again from there.

Later, the bill has one coffee and one tea on it. I check the numbers and pay my part.

Outside, the river is grey. A child is trying to fly a small kite. It falls into the grass. He picks it up and runs again.

“Same time next week?” Rui asks.

“Maybe. Send me a message.”

I put my phone away. We walk towards the station, and this time I know which exit to use.

### 开口练习

#### 实用表达

- **Excuse me.** — 礼貌地引起注意
- **I ordered a hot tea.** — 说明原来的请求
- **Could I have tea instead?** — 提出更换要求
- **Do I need to pay for both?** — 确认费用
- **What happened after the test?** — 从没听懂的节点继续追问

#### 语言与发音

I ordered 表示之前已经点过。用 I ordered… / This is… / Could I have… instead? 组成三句说明问题，不用先想复杂时态。

强调对比：I ordered TEA. This is COFFEE。说清两个名词，比整句加快更重要。

#### 理解问题

1. Why doesn’t Andy hear the drinks clearly?
2. Does Rui speak to the waiter for Andy?
3. Does Andy have to pay for both drinks?

#### 角色扮演

你点了热茶，服务员送来冰咖啡。

> Here’s your coffee.

你的任务：礼貌引起注意，说你点的是热茶。

> I have coffee on the order.

你的任务：不指责对方，请求改成茶。

> Okay. I’ll bring you tea.

你的任务：询问是否需要付两杯的钱。

#### 换个场景

换成商店：你买蓝色杯子，袋子里却是白色的。用三句请求更换并确认是否额外收费。

#### 脱稿复述

讲述这次点餐误会。包括 Andy 最初想怎么做、后来实际说了什么、结果如何。

#### 理解题参考答案（先自己回答）

1. A loud machine starts in the kitchen.
2. No. He waits and lets Andy speak.
3. No. He only pays for the tea.

## Chapter 5 — Not Tonight

A2 · 道歉、表达边界、提出补救安排

Two weeks later, Rui helps me carry a second-hand desk lamp home. The lamp is cheap, but heavy. We take turns holding it on the stairs.

“Tea?” I ask outside our door.

“Yes. Thanks.”

I open the door. The apartment is dark except for a light under Maya’s bedroom door.

I put the lamp in my room. Rui sits by the window. We start talking about a film, first quietly, then louder. He tells me about a strange scene. I laugh.

Maya opens her door. She is wearing headphones.

“Oh, hi, Rui.”

“Hi.”

She looks at me.

“Andy, can we talk for a minute?”

We go into the kitchen.

“I’m on a work call,” she says. “I can hear you in my room.”

“Sorry. We can be quiet.”

“I need the apartment to be quiet tonight. I have another call after this one.”

I look at the cups in my hands.

“Rui helped me carry something. I only asked him in for tea.”

“I understand. But you didn’t ask me.”

Her voice is tired. Mine gets a little louder.

“I live here too.”

“Yes,” she says. “You do.”

Neither of us speaks. From the other room, I hear Rui move his chair.

Maya takes off her headphones.

“I’m not saying you can’t have friends here. I’m asking you to check first when it’s late.”

I look at the clock. It is nearly nine. We spent longer in the lamp shop than I thought.

“Okay,” I say. “I’m sorry about the noise.”

“Thank you.”

“We can go to the café downstairs.”

“That would help.”

Before she leaves the kitchen, I ask another question.

“What time is late?”

She stops.

“Good question. After eight on a work night?”

“Can you check with me too?”

“Yes. Of course.”

“And on weekends?”

“Let’s talk about that tomorrow. I really need to get back.”

I nod. This time, I know what I am agreeing to.

In the living room, Rui is already putting on his jacket.

“Shall we go?” he asks.

“Yes. Sorry.”

“Don’t worry. I need food more than tea.”

Downstairs, we order two bowls of noodles. I tell Rui I feel bad.

“About the noise?”

“And about asking you to leave.”

“You’re coming with me,” he says. “It’s fine.”

The next morning, Maya is at the kitchen table. There is coffee in her blue cup.

“Thanks for last night,” she says.

“Sorry again.”

“We should make a plan. For both of us.”

We agree to send a message before bringing visitors in the evening. If one of us needs quiet, we will say so. On weekends, we will check when someone wants to stay late.

Maya looks at the lamp in my room.

“That’s nice.”

“It’s very heavy.”

“Then I’m glad Rui helped.”

I switch it on. The light falls across my empty desk.

“Would you like some tea?” I ask.

“Not now. I’m going out.”

“Okay. See you later.”

I make one cup and take it to my room.

### 开口练习

#### 实用表达

- **Can we talk for a minute?** — 提出简短沟通
- **I’m sorry about the noise.** — 为具体行为道歉
- **I need some quiet tonight.** — 说明自己的边界
- **We can go downstairs.** — 提出可执行的补救
- **Can you check with me too?** — 请求双方遵守同样规则

#### 语言与发音

I’m sorry about + 名词：the noise / the mess / the delay。选一个真实小问题道歉，再加一句补救办法。

练习平静说 I need some QUIET tonight，避免把每个词都重读。再说 I’m sorry about the NOISE，让重点清楚。

#### 理解问题

1. Why does Rui come to the apartment?
2. Why does Maya need quiet?
3. What do Andy and Maya agree to do?

#### 角色扮演

室友告诉你声音太大。先理解问题，再商量办法。

> I’m working. I can hear you from my room.

你的任务：为噪声道歉，不急着解释。

> I need quiet for another hour.

你的任务：提出和朋友到楼下去。

> Please ask before bringing people late at night.

你的任务：询问几点算晚，并请对方也提前沟通。

#### 换个场景

换成同事在你旁边打电话：你要参加线上面试。用两三句说明你需要安静，并商量一个时间范围。

#### 脱稿复述

先说双方各自需要什么，再讲解决办法。尽量不用 good person / bad person 来评价人物。

#### 理解题参考答案（先自己回答）

1. He helps Andy carry a heavy desk lamp.
2. She is on a work call and has another call afterwards.
3. Send a message before bringing visitors in the evening and say when they need quiet.

## Chapter 6 — The Message from Work

A2–B1 · 核对重要信息、表达感受、说明下一步

A few weeks after I move in, I arrive at work and find a message on my screen.

“Team meeting. Ten o’clock. Please come to the meeting room.”

There is no list of topics.

Our manager, Wei, is already there. Usually, he has coffee. Today, he has a glass of water and several sheets of paper.

The meeting is in Chinese. I understand every word. For once, language is not the problem.

The company is closing our team. There is not enough work for everyone. Our last working day will be at the end of next month. We will receive the details in writing.

Someone asks about pay. Someone else asks about moving to another team. Wei answers slowly. There are no other positions for us at the moment.

I look at the small mark on the table beside my hand. I have seen it in many meetings. I have never looked at it this closely.

“Do you have any questions?” Wei asks.

I shake my head.

Back at my desk, I open the same file three times. I cannot remember what I want to change.

At lunch, I send Rui a message.

“My team is closing.”

He calls a minute later.

“Are you okay?”

“I don’t know.”

“When do you finish?”

“At the end of next month.”

“Do you have the details?”

“They’re sending them.”

“Read them carefully. We can talk tonight.”

I say yes, then ask what time.

When I get home, Maya is cutting vegetables. There is room for both of us in the kitchen now.

“How was work?” she asks.

I put my bag on the floor.

“Not good.”

She puts down the knife.

“What happened?”

I know the words in Chinese. In English, I can only find small pieces.

“My team is closing. Next month. I need a new job.”

“Oh, Andy. I’m sorry.”

I sit down.

“Did you know this was coming?”

“No. We had less work. But I didn’t know.”

“Do you want to talk, or do you want some food first?”

“Food, please.”

She takes another plate from the cupboard. We eat rice and vegetables. For a while, we talk about nothing important. She tells me the shop downstairs has new opening hours. I tell her the lift makes a strange sound.

After dinner, I open my banking app. I have some savings. I can pay my part of the rent for several months, but I need to spend less.

“I can still pay the rent,” I tell Maya.

“Okay. Thank you for telling me.”

“I’ll look for work this weekend.”

“What kind of work?”

“Software. Maybe an international company.”

I almost laugh after saying it.

“My English isn’t good enough.”

“For every job?” she asks.

“I don’t know.”

“Then look at a few. Find out what they need.”

She does not promise to find me a job. She does not say everything will be easy. She gets her laptop and begins her own work.

At eight, Rui calls. I take the call in my room, under the light of my new lamp.

Before we finish, I write three things on a piece of paper: read the company letter, update my CV, choose three jobs to look at.

# Part 2 — A Different Answer

## Chapter 7 — What Do You Do?

A2–B1 · 清楚介绍自己的工作与能力

The woman across the table turns her paper cup between her hands. Behind her, someone opens the café door, and cold air moves around our feet.

“So, Andy, what do you do?” she asks.

My team closed a month ago. Since then, my days have filled with job websites, applications, and careful trips to the supermarket.

I know this question. I have practised an answer on the metro. But now I look at the wet mark under my cup.

“I'm between jobs,” I say.

“Oh.” She waits.

“My team closed.”

She looks sorry, and I want to change the subject. Before I can ask about her work, Rui returns with three glasses of water.

“Andy builds software,” he says. “We met through Maya.”

The woman leans forward. Her name is Jo. Rui met her at a local technology event, and today she is joining us for coffee. She manages a small training business. We use English because it is the language everyone at our table shares.

“What kind of software?” she asks.

“Business systems. Things people use at work.”

I stop again. My answer sounds like a box with nothing inside it.

“Can you give me an example?”

I put my cup down. “At my last company, I worked on a stock system. A shop could see how many products it had.”

“Like how many blue shirts?”

“Yes. Exactly. Sometimes the number was wrong because two people changed it at the same time. I helped fix that.”

Jo nods. “That sounds useful. We have a similar problem with room bookings.”

For ten minutes, we talk about two teachers trying to use one room. She draws two small squares on a paper napkin. I ask how the teachers book rooms now. I understand her answer. I even forget to worry about the word I cannot remember.

Then she asks, “Are you looking for the same kind of role?”

“Yes, but perhaps in an international team.”

“Why?”

The easy answer is better pay. That is part of it. My rent does not stop when my job stops. But there is another reason.

“I want to work with people in different places. And I want to learn how other teams build things.”

Rui drinks his water. He does not add anything this time.

When Jo leaves, she takes the napkin with her. She does not offer me a job. I do not expect one. Still, our conversation feels different from the forms I fill in alone.

On the metro home, I open one of those forms on my phone. Under ‘Current position’, there is an empty box. I have looked at it three times today.

At home, Maya sits at the kitchen table with a large sheet of paper and several colour samples.

“Can I use this end?” I ask.

“Of course. Please keep drinks away from these.”

I put my tea on the kitchen counter. Then I open my laptop and write ‘Software developer’ in a draft of my introduction. Under it, I describe the stock problem in three short sentences.

Maya moves a green sample beside a grey one.

“How was coffee?” she asks.

“I talked about my work.”

“Good coffee conversation?”

“Yes. Better than my job applications.”

She smiles, but her phone rings before she can answer. She takes the call into her room and closes the door.

I read my three sentences aloud, quietly. The second sentence is too long. I cut it in two.

Beside my laptop is the budget I made this morning. I still need a job. But when the form asks what I do, I finally have something clear to put in the box.

- **Can I use this end?** — 询问共用空间的使用权限

## Chapter 8 — A Small Favour

A2–B1 · 提出有限、具体的帮助请求

Two weeks after coffee with Rui and Jo, I find a role that interests me. Maya is washing a lunch container when I come into the kitchen with my laptop.

“Could you look at my CV?” I ask. “Just a small favour.”

She turns off the tap. It no longer leaks, but I still sometimes expect to hear a drop.

“How long is it?”

“Four pages.”

“And when do you need it?”

“Tonight, if possible.”

She looks at the laptop, then at the clock above the fridge.

“I can't do tonight. I have work to finish.”

“Only the English,” I say quickly.

“That can take quite a while, Andy.”

My hands tighten around the computer. “Right. Sorry.”

“I can give you twenty minutes tomorrow, after dinner. Choose the part you most want help with.”

I say yes. This time I understand exactly what I am agreeing to.

The next afternoon, I go to the public library near our apartment. A child at the next table is drawing a bus. I open my CV and look at its four pages. Every small task from my old job is there. Some sentences are longer than the bus in the child's picture.

I save a copy before changing anything. Then I remove old school details and several lines about tools I hardly use. I keep the work that shows what I can do.

Near the top, I have written, ‘Excellent communication skills in an international environment.’ I look at it for a long time. I copied the style from a sample CV. But I have never worked in an international team.

I delete the sentence.

By dinner, I have two pages and three questions. Maya brings her bowl to the sink and sets a timer on her phone.

“Where shall we start?” she asks.

“The introduction. And this example about the stock system.”

She reads without speaking. I watch her face and try not to explain every line.

“What does ‘responsible for improvement’ mean here?” she asks.

“I found a problem and changed part of the system.”

“Then say that. What problem?”

“When two workers saved new numbers together, one change could disappear.”

“Did you fix it alone?”

“No. Another developer checked my plan, and our tester helped us check the result.”

I write that down. The new version is longer, but it tells the truth.

Maya points to another sentence. “You say you led the project. Were you the project lead?”

“Not officially. I handled that part.”

“Then perhaps say which part you handled.”

I move the cursor. For a moment, I want to keep the stronger word. Other applicants probably have better English and more important titles. Then I imagine someone asking me to explain that title in an interview.

I change it.

The timer makes a soft sound. Maya puts her hands on the edge of the table.

“I need to stop now.”

“There is one more page,” I begin. Then I close it. “Thanks. I can work on the rest.”

She gathers her things. “I can help with clear writing, but I don't know which technical details an employer needs. Rui may know more about that.”

After she leaves, I check the job advertisement myself. It asks for experience with business software, solving problems, and working with a team. I mark one example for each.

I want to send the application immediately, but first I read every date and company name. At the bottom, I find an old phone number.

I replace it with the right one, save the file, and attach it to my application. The company is called Meridian. Before I press send, I check the attachment once more.

#### 实用表达

- **Could you look at my CV?** — 提出具体请求
- **When do you need it?** — 确认截止时间
- **I can give you twenty minutes.** — 说明能投入的时间
- **Which part did you handle?** — 核实实际职责
- **I need to stop now.** — 清楚结束一次帮助

#### 语言与发音

用 can 表达可提供的帮助和边界：I can give you twenty minutes tomorrow. 将时间和范围说具体，再用 I can’t… 说明限制。

#### 理解问题

1. How much time does Maya offer?
2. Why does Andy remove the sentence about international communication?
3. What error does Andy find before sending his CV?

#### 角色扮演

你扮演 Andy，请忙碌的室友看一小段申请材料。

> I have twenty minutes tomorrow. What do you need?

你的任务：接受时限，指定最需要帮助的一段。

> Did you lead this whole project?

你的任务：准确说明自己的职责，不夸大。

> I need to get back to my work now.

你的任务：感谢帮助，并说明剩余部分由自己完成。

#### 换个场景

换到工作场景：同事请你今天检查十页材料，但你只有十五分钟。开口提出一个做得到的范围和时间，再说明其余部分如何安排。

## Chapter 9 — Tell Me About a Time

A2–B1 · 用简短结构讲工作经历

Three weeks after I send my application, I arrive for an interview. The meeting room at Meridian is smaller than I expect. Through the glass wall, I can see a row of coats and a printer with a red light.

A woman named Sara sits opposite me. A technical manager, Ben, joins on a screen. His first words arrive without sound. Sara touches a button, and suddenly his voice fills the room.

“Can you hear me now?”

“Yes,” I say. That question is easy.

We begin with my recent work. I explain the stock system. Ben asks how I stopped one change from replacing another. I draw a simple example on the paper Sara gives me. He asks another question, and I answer it. For a few minutes, this feels like work I already know.

Then Sara folds her hands.

“Tell me about a time when a project didn't go as planned.”

I prepared for this question. My answer is in a notebook inside my bag. At home, it takes two minutes. Here, I cannot find the first sentence in my head.

“We had a problem,” I say. “A large problem. But we worked hard and finished.”

“What was the problem?”

“A report. It was slow.”

“Could you tell us what you did?”

I start from the beginning. I describe the company, the team, and the first version of the report. I tell them which month we began. Ben looks down at something outside the screen.

I speak faster.

After a while, Sara gently interrupts. “And what was your own part?”

There it is again: the thing I have still not explained.

“I checked the slow part,” I say. “I changed how we read the data.”

“Did that solve it?”

“Partly. We also made the first release smaller.”

“Who decided that?”

“Our manager, after I showed the test results.”

Sara writes something. I wish I had started with that sentence.

Later, Ben describes a situation with an unhappy customer and a late delivery. There are two words I do not know. I understand ‘customer’ and ‘Friday’, so I answer about finishing by Friday.

He pauses. “Actually, I'm asking what you would do if Friday was no longer possible.”

My face feels hot. “Sorry. I misunderstood.”

I ask him to repeat the question. This time, I say I would check the remaining work and tell the manager what was possible. The answer is short, but at least it answers his question.

When the interview ends, Sara explains that the recruitment team will contact me about the next step. I thank them, take my bag, and walk past the printer. Its red light is still on.

Outside, I stand beside a tree before opening my umbrella. People move around me toward the metro.

My phone has a message from Rui: ‘How did it go?’

I type, ‘Terrible.’ Then I remove the word.

‘Some technical questions were fine. I got lost in one answer.’

He replies while I am on the train. ‘Which answer?’

That evening, we talk on the phone. I explain the slow report, including everything about the first version.

“Wait,” Rui says. “I already know your old company. What did you do?”

I laugh, though it is not very funny.

I try again. “The report was too slow. I measured the problem, changed part of it, and showed the results. We reduced the first release.”

“What happened after that?”

“The smaller report worked. We added the other parts later.”

“Tell them that next time.”

After the call, I open my notebook. I do not write a beautiful new speech. I write four short lines, leaving space between them. Then I close the notebook and try to tell the story without looking.

- **What was your own part?** — 追问个人贡献
- **I misunderstood.** — 承认理解有误
- **Could you repeat the question?** — 请求重复问题
- **We made the first release smaller.** — 说明缩小首版范围的决定

#### 语言与发音

用一般过去时讲完成的工作：I measured the problem and changed part of the system. 按问题、行动、结果组织三句，注意 worked、changed 与不规则动词 made。

#### 理解问题

1. Which part of the interview goes well at first?
2. What is missing from the beginning of his project story?
3. What does Ben actually ask about Friday?

你扮演 Andy，在模拟面试中讲一个真实的技术问题。

你的任务：先说问题，控制在两句内。

> What did you personally do?

你的任务：用过去时说自己的两项行动。

> And what happened in the end?

你的任务：说结果，并交代是否还有工作留到以后。

#### 换个场景

## Chapter 10 — The Call I Missed

A2–B1 · 回电并核实时间与目的

My phone lights up beside a bag of noodles. By the time I put down the vegetables and take off my gloves, the ringing stops.

There is a number I do not recognise and a voice message.

I stand near the supermarket window and press play. A woman says her name, something about Meridian, and then several words disappear under the sound of a passing bus.

My stomach tightens. Perhaps they want another interview. Perhaps they have already chosen someone else.

I listen again. This time I hear, “arrange a short feedback call”. There is a name at the end: Lucy.

At home, I put the frozen food away first. Then I sit at the table with my phone. Calling back should be simple. I have made hundreds of calls about work in Chinese. But in English, I cannot see where the other person's sentence is going.

I write Lucy's name on a piece of paper. Under it, I write three things: my name, missed call, feedback time.

Maya comes in carrying a long cardboard tube.

“Big delivery?” I ask.

“Presentation prints.” She puts the tube by her bedroom door. “Everything okay?”

“Meridian called. I missed it.”

“Will you call back?”

“Yes. I'm just getting ready.”

She goes into her room. I look at my three lines once more and press the number.

“Good afternoon, Meridian recruitment.”

“Hello. This is Andy. I'm returning a call from Lucy about my interview.”

“One moment, please.”

Music starts. I breathe out. That part is done.

Lucy comes on the line and thanks me for calling. She speaks quickly. I hear ‘Thursday’, ‘ten’, and ‘fifteen’.

“Yes,” I begin. Then I stop. “Sorry, do you mean ten fifteen, or a fifteen-minute call?”

“A fifteen-minute call at ten on Thursday.”

I write it down. “Thursday at ten in the morning, Shanghai time?”

“That's right. I'll send you a calendar invitation.”

“Thank you. Is there anything I should prepare?”

“No preparation is needed. Sara would like to share feedback from the interview.”

I look at the word ‘feedback’ on my page. It does not tell me if the news is good.

“Will you discuss the decision then?” I ask.

“Yes, Sara will explain the outcome.”

I thank Lucy and end the call. My hand is warm around the phone. We spoke for less than three minutes, but I feel as if I have walked up several floors.

The calendar invitation arrives. I check the date, time, and meeting link against my notes. They match. I accept it and add a reminder.

For the rest of the afternoon, I want to read meaning into every word Lucy used. She sounded friendly. Was that a good sign? The call is short. Is that a bad sign?

I open another job advertisement. After reading the same line three times, I close it and make lunch.

Maya returns to the kitchen for water.

“Did you reach them?”

“Yes. Feedback on Thursday.”

“Okay.”

“I don't know what it means.”

She fills her glass. “It means you have a call on Thursday.”

Her answer is almost too simple. But she is right about what we actually know.

I eat my noodles while she checks a message. Then she goes back to her prints, and I return to the advertisement.

Before I start reading, I turn the phone's sound on. I place it beside the laptop, away from the edge. The reminder is set. There is nothing else to do about Thursday today.

你的任务：复述日期、时刻和时长，确认时区。

## Chapter 11 — Not This Time

At nine fifty-eight on Thursday, I join the call. My glass of water sits beside the laptop. Outside our apartment, someone is moving a chair across the floor.

“Thank you for making time, Andy.”

“Are you applying?” she asks.

“Yes. But I'm checking the role carefully first.”

She hands me a dry plate. I put it in the cupboard, then return for the next one. My laptop waits at the far end of the table, with the new advertisement still open.

## Chapter 14 — What Does “Done” Mean?

It takes longer than typing done. Daniel replies with a thumbs-up and moves his demonstration to the smaller scope.

Before I leave, I take one final look at the task. The mark is still grey. For once, the colour is telling the truth.

## Chapter 17 — I Should Have Asked

B1–B2 · 承担判断失误，区分事实、修复和后续影响

Six messages arrive in the test inbox while I am looking at a single class. Each subject line announces the same new time. I refresh the page, hoping that the inbox is showing one message several times.

It is not.

Rui stands behind my chair. We have been running an internal test of repeated class changes. The connection problem from last month has been resolved, and the test service now provides the records we need. Today should have been straightforward.

“How many times did you change it?” he asks.

“Three.”

“Then why are there six emails?”

I open the logs. My chest feels tight before I find the answer.

The first change creates one notification. The second sends two. The third sends three. Each time, my code creates a new job from the earlier change records as well as the current one.

I wrote it that way. I remember thinking that learners might need another reminder of earlier changes. I did not ask anyone whether that was wanted. I did not properly consider how the same path would run each time a class was edited.

“Stop the test,” I say.

Rui closes the test screen. I pause the notification jobs for our internal environment. Then we check the addresses against the test account list. Every message has gone to an inbox controlled by our team. No real learner or centre staff member has received one.

That matters. It does not make the code right.

Daniel joins us after reading Rui's message. “Is this the email service sending twice?”

“No,” I say. The word comes out too sharply. I try again. “No. It's my change logic. I create jobs for previous changes again. I assumed we wanted repeated notices without checking.”

“Who received them?”

“Only our internal test accounts. We've checked the recipients.”

“And is it still running?”

“I've paused these test jobs.”

He pulls over a chair. “Show me.”

I show him the three actions and six messages. We write down the times, the affected test class and the number of jobs. Rui checks my explanation against the logs rather than accepting it because I sound certain.

Daniel asks how long the repair will take.

“The code change may be small,” I say. “I need to check the rule with Elena and test repeated edits before I estimate the whole repair.”

He looks at the calendar. “We were planning to finish this review tomorrow.”

“I know.”

I want to offer to work all night. It would sound like taking responsibility. It would also be another promise made before I understand the work.

We send Elena a short explanation of the internal failure and a specific question about repeated changes. Her reply is clear: each saved time change should produce its own notification showing that change. Earlier changes should not be sent again simply because staff edit the class later. Repeating a failed delivery is a separate technical case, not a new change notice.

With that rule written down, Rui and I work through the fix. I remove the loop over earlier changes from the new-change path. We add checks for two and three consecutive edits, and for a job that is attempted again after a temporary failure. We also check that saving the same unchanged time does not create another change notice.

The first new test fails. I have fixed the normal path but missed another place where jobs are created. Rui points to it without speaking. I feel my face heat up again and make the second correction.

By late afternoon, the automated checks pass. We reset the test class and repeat the internal exercise together. Three saved changes produce three notices with the expected times. Repeating the same job does not add a duplicate message in our controlled test.

Daniel moves the review by two days so another engineer can examine the change and the wider notification flow. Passing our checks is useful evidence; it is not the end of the investigation.

That evening, I open my notebook at the kitchen table. Maya is reading on the sofa.

“I made a mistake today,” I say.

She looks up. “Do you want to talk, or do you need quiet?”

“Quiet first.”

“All right.”

I write the incident note without the sentence 'I was only trying to help'. I include what happened, what we checked, what changed and what still needs review. My hands are steady now, although I do not feel especially confident. I save the note and put the laptop away.

## Chapter 18 — A Different Plan

Each option has something uncomfortable attached. A broad introduction means more staff need help at once. A pilot means someone must coordinate two processes and keep the records consistent. A delay gives us preparation time, but staff continue copying changes between lists, and the client waits longer for the work they have paid for.

I cannot make the pilot win simply by leaving its costs off the page.

“What would make us stop?”

Daniel draws a line under that point.

“No. We'd have to test it and agree who makes the decision.”

He looks out through the glass wall. Someone is carrying a monitor past, walking backwards while a colleague holds the other end.

“All right,” he says. “Let's evaluate the one-centre approach with Elena. I'm not approving a launch date today.”

“I'll make that clear in the notes.”

“And send her the alternatives. She may have reasons we haven't considered.”

After the meeting, I place the page beside my keyboard and open a blank document for questions. Who has time to support a pilot? How do staff reach learners who cannot use the new system? Which existing records must remain?

I send the discussion paper and wait for Elena's response. On the whiteboard, all the centre names are still there. Beside them, Daniel has added a second question: 'What would one centre need?'

## Chapter 21 — The Cost of Friday

B2–C1 · 说明选择、成本与必要条件

“Yes,” Rui says. “But these conditions aren't about having every feature. They're about staff being able to use the limited version safely.”

He looks surprised, then checks his calendar. “Half past ten.”

“Can it happen over cold noodles?”

“No.”

“Then perhaps eat first.”

I put the phone on the kitchen counter. The noodles are already sticking together. I separate them with my chopsticks while Maya tells me about a client who has asked for a fifth version of the same page.

## Chapter 22 — A Difficult Conversation

B2–C1 · 区分意图与影响，协商工作边界

At ten twenty-eight, I stand outside the small meeting room with a notebook that contains three opening sentences. All three sound reasonable on paper. None sounds like something I can say without reading.

Daniel arrives carrying coffee and a charging cable. “I've got half an hour,” he says.

“That should be enough.”

“I want to explain what happened for me yesterday,” I begin. “When you said you needed more than reasons the pilot might go wrong, I felt that the choices I'd prepared weren't being considered.”

He looks down at his cup. “I was frustrated.”

“I could tell. I also became less willing to explain the remaining risks. That concerns me, because explaining them is part of my job.”

The room becomes very quiet. In the corridor, a trolley wheel makes a repeated squeak. I resist the urge to soften everything I have said with a quick apology.

Daniel takes the lid off his coffee. “I didn't mean that you should hide risks.”

“I don't think you did. I'm describing the effect, not guessing your intention.”

He nods once. “Fair.”

I had imagined a longer disagreement. The small agreement leaves me briefly without a next sentence.

“What I need,” he continues, “is a recommendation that I can take to the client and defend. The budget is limited. The centre has made arrangements. If all I say is that engineering isn't comfortable, I haven't given anyone a decision they can act on.”

“I agree. I recommend the smaller supervised pilot, provided we meet the essential checks. If we don't, I recommend a delay.”

“That still sounds conditional.”

“It is conditional. I can't honestly remove the conditions before we've checked them.”

He leans back. I can see the beginning of an answer, and then a decision to wait.

I explain the checks one by one: staff access, correct handling of a class-time change, the notification tests, a practised fallback, and a named person who can stop the pilot. We do not need every report or every improvement. We do need the people at the desk to know what happens when a step fails.

Daniel asks which checks are complete and which are not. This question is easier. I show him the evidence and the remaining owners. He points out that one owner has not agreed to the deadline. He is right. I circle the name instead of defending the document.

Then he asks, “Has the internal test problem made you too cautious?”

My first reaction is to deny it. I can still see the repeated emails in the test inbox. I remember explaining that I had assumed a new saved change should also send notices for earlier changes again. Nobody had asked me to make that assumption.

“It has made me more cautious,” I say. “I'm not sure every part of that caution is useful. But the duplicate-message checks are based on a specific failure. The staff-access concern comes from Priya. They aren't all the same kind of worry.”

“That's helpful.” He turns the notebook towards himself. “We should separate evidence from anxiety. Including mine.”

I look up.

“I worry that another delay will damage the client's trust,” he says. “I also worry about using the whole budget before we learn anything. Sometimes I push for a date because a date makes everyone organise the work.”

“And sometimes I stay quiet because I think you'll decide I'm difficult.”

“Then I assume you've agreed.”

We both stop. It is an ordinary exchange, with no dramatic revelation, but it describes months of small misunderstandings better than either of our documents.

We agree on a practical change. When a decision affects my work, I will state agreement, disagreement, or what I still need to check. Daniel will ask for that response rather than treating the absence of an objection as approval. Neither of us promises to do it perfectly.

He asks for the revised recommendation by mid-afternoon. I say I can complete it if the reporting task moves to tomorrow. He checks the schedule and accepts the trade-off.

As I gather my notebook, he says, “You were right to ask for this meeting.”

“Next time I'd prefer to ask before I'm upset.”

“That would probably help both of us.”

At my desk, I contact the person whose name I circled. The deadline is not workable. We find an earlier, smaller piece of preparation they can complete and make the remaining dependency visible. It is a less impressive line in the plan, but a more accurate one.

Rui passes with an empty mug. “How did it go?”

“We agreed on what I need to recommend.”

“Did he agree with you?”

“On some things.”

Rui considers this, then puts his mug down to read the new decision note. I move my chair so he can see the screen.

## Chapter 23 — Good Enough to Launch?

“One learner has. She was polite, but she asked whether the class had changed again.”

At the end of my support period, I hand the current status to Daniel, who has arranged the remaining cover. I do not stay online simply to prove that I care. He reads back the open issue and the next action, and I close my laptop.

When someone asks whether the pilot was a success, Daniel looks at the notes.

“It was useful,” he says. “And we have work to do.”

I save that version of the report. Outside, the rain has stopped, and the pavement is bright under the office lights.

## Chapter 24 — The Empty Chair

“Tuesday works. Put it in the calendar.”

Before leaving, I write to Elena about an unresolved question in the next proposal. I start with a long sentence that sounds impressive but says very little. I delete it and write three shorter ones: what we know, what we do not know, and what decision we need from her.

At apartment 4B, I find Maya kneeling beside a cardboard tube. A large printed board leans against the sofa.

“Careful with your shoes,” she says. “There's a tiny screw somewhere, and I would like to find it before either of us does with a foot.”

“I can manage that.”

We make dinner from what each of us has agreed to share. The kitchen tap closes properly. There is no bucket beneath it. I still remember standing there on my first evening, pretending to understand a repair time that would have made me late for work.

“I'll clear that.”

“Was your new colleague comfortable?” she asks.

“I don't know yet. He asked a useful question.”

She nods. I pull out the empty chair and sit down.

你扮演 Andy，面对新同事和自己的持续学习需要，表达自然且不过度承担。

> I reviewed the guide, but I’m still thinking.

你的任务：允许稍后补充，不逼对方马上表达

> Can you spend a whole afternoon showing Sam the system?

你的任务：提出可用时间，并说明更多时间需要调整任务

> Do you understand the dependency now?

你的任务：明确已理解部分，针对仍不清楚的先后关系提问

#### 换个场景

可选 IELTS 风格口语拓展：用英语回答 “What makes a good colleague?”，讲一个具体例子，再讨论为什么自信不等于总是发言。录音两分钟，回听时只检查例子与观点是否连贯，不给自己模拟分数。

#### 脱稿复述

脱稿讲三分钟的全书收尾：从初到合租屋的误解，讲到能提问、表达边界、邀请别人参与；用最后餐桌场景结束，同时保留 Andy 仍需练习的事实。

#### 理解题参考答案（先自己回答）

1. Silence can have many meanings, and Andy knows little about him.
2. She has asked to present the research section at her team review.
3. Books occupy the chair again, but Andy now clears it himself and joins the conversation at the table.
